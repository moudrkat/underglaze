why there are no sines

Put the origin on the tile's four-fold centre. A real image with C4 symmetry there has purely real Fourier coefficients — reality gives F(−k) = conj F(k), C4 gives F(−k) = F(k), and nothing imaginary survives. Largest imaginary part found: 2.6 × 10−17.

what the firing does
mt+1 = [ Gℓ ∗ mt > ½ ] Blur, then re-threshold — the Merriman–Bence–Osher scheme (1992). It converges to motion by mean curvature.

Generations 0, 1, 3, 8, 20, 60. Perimeter 40 744 → 6 210; 166 ink regions → 13.

A real firing is the first three steps of that third slider — across the plausible Ea, one high firing gives ℓ between 0.007 and 0.29 mm. One firing barely touches the tile. Curvature flow has no non-trivial fixed point, so left alone it does not preserve a pattern, it removes one.

is it a fractal?

Someone asked. A fractal is a shape that does not care how far you zoom in — a snowflake edge has the same notches at every magnification. That is measurable. Lay a grid of boxes of side ε over the outline, count how many boxes it touches, shrink the boxes, count again:

D  =  limε→0   log N(ε)  /  log(1/ε) Halve the box and a straight line needs twice as many boxes, so D = 1. A filled area needs four times, so D = 2. A fractal falls in between — and gives back the same D at every ε. That last part is the whole test.

So measure D separately at each zoom rather than fitting one number across all of them:

Box-counting dimension, one zoom level at a time. A fractal holds one value. The tile does not — and neither does the dashed line, which is this same tile rebuilt from cosines, and therefore made of provably smooth curves.

The tile climbs from 1.0 to 1.9, tracking the smooth control the whole way. That climb is not roughness, it is the boxes getting big enough to cover everything. Fit one number across all zooms at once and you get 1.34, which looks satisfyingly fractal and means nothing, because the guaranteed-smooth control gives it too.

Second test, the same one the mirrors got. Zooming is a symmetry like any other, so correlate the tile against a magnified view of itself around the four-fold centre:

Not a fractal, and it could not have been one: p4 is a crystallographic group — whole-tile shifts and a quarter turn, nothing else. It contains no zoom. And f is a finite sum of cosines, so the line between blue and white is an ordinary smooth curve, for any number of terms.

Then build one, to check the ruler

A measurement that only ever says no is not yet trustworthy. The tile fails for one narrow reason — the series stops. Remove exactly that, by adding the motif back at every zoom with each copy weaker by a fixed ratio, and nothing else changes:

fH(x)  =  Σj   b−jH   f(bjx) Weierstrass, in two dimensions. b is a whole number and the grid is odd, so f(bjx) is exact rather than interpolated: the period and the four-fold centre survive every level. Still a sum of cosines, still p4 — now with a dilation symmetry it did not have. H is the knob, and the contour pattern of such a field has D = 2 − H.

The knob is how far the boundary is allowed to wander. At 0 px it is the tile. Every step adds the same wobble at every scale at once — that is what scale-free means.

Flat, and sitting on Koch's line, at the 2 − H = 1.30 the construction was asked for. The ruler works: it refuses the tile and accepts this. That is the only reason this exists.

Two things it cost. The first attempt added copies across the whole plane, including the middle of solid areas, where a perturbation moves the boundary by (perturbation ÷ steepness) and the interior is flat — so it punched holes and the drawing dissolved into mould. Weighting by |∇f| fixes it: the edge frays, the interior does not move.

Fractal, D ≈ 1.3, and no longer a tile.

The same construction weighted by |∇f|.

The second is not fixable and is the point. Scale-free means the wobble at scale L is (L/L₀)H times the wobble at the top, so frilling at 2 px cannot be ordered without 27H times as much of it at 256 px. Fine detail is not an accessory bolted on the bottom. Asking for it only at the bottom is asking for a cutoff — which is what made the tile not a fractal in the first place.

an onion pattern with no onion in it

Zwiebelmuster has five motifs: bamboo, aster, peony, peach, and the “onion”. Audited against a marked Meissen (Teichert) plate, this tile has one of them.

Motif list and audit: the five canonical Zwiebelmuster motifs as given by the Meissen Porcelain Museum, compared element by element against a marked Meissen (Teichert) plate and against this tile. Correlations by src/symmetry.py.

It kept the flowers and dropped every fruit — the motifs the pattern is named for. Not Zwiebelmuster, then: a derivative, redrawn for printing and rearranged four-fold.

The tile itself is Czechoslovak, 15 × 15 cm, 4 mm. An identical one is sold second-hand as původní obkládačky vzor cibulák; in Czech that name covers the whole blue-on-white floral family. Correlated against it: rotations +0.238, mirrors +0.097 — same tile, same handedness.
